# Research basis

This document records the rationale and limits of Mentor's tutoring decisions. The operating instructions are contained in `mentor/SKILL.md`; the README describes their use. Research explanations are not prerequisites for using the skill.

The decision structure connects a performance criterion, learner work, feedback, and a subsequent attempt. The subsequent attempt informs the next support decision. Sadler (1989) and Hattie & Timperley (2007) support this use of assessment to guide improvement; van de Pol et al. (2010) identify contingency, fading, and transfer of responsibility as characteristics of scaffolding. Combining these ideas into this particular protocol is a design synthesis, not evidence that the combination outperforms its components.

## Instructional decisions

Each row maps a node in the skill to its basis and an observable check. Research findings support the stated principles under studied conditions; their translation into these instructions is a design decision.

| Node | Research basis and applicability | Decision and observable check |
|---|---|---|
| Define the target | Sadler (1989) addresses standards, comparison, and improvement in judgments using multiple criteria. Hattie & Timperley (2007) connect goals, current performance, and next steps. | A task has observable success criteria. A response can be compared with those criteria, including acceptable alternatives for tasks without one correct answer. |
| Map the task | Merrill (2002) synthesizes task-centered instruction, activation of prior knowledge, demonstration, application, and integration. Pollock et al. (2002) studied separate elements followed by their interactions; Kalyuga et al. (2003) qualify instruction by prior knowledge. | The current goal determines the necessary knowledge, actions, decisions, and prerequisites. A unit is small enough to attempt and assess, but retains its role in the whole task. Existing work permits skipping components; uncertain components receive a diagnostic attempt or appropriate instruction. Units can be revised and recombined. This minimal-unit rule is a design heuristic, not a proved optimum or a validated decomposition method for every domain. |
| Assess the attempt | Hattie & Timperley (2007) distinguish feedback about tasks, processes, self-regulation, and the person. Kalyuga et al. (2003) describe interactions between expertise and instructional guidance. | Existing work informs the next task. For a numerical error, the skill calls for checking the reference result, the difference, and the step responsible while preserving correct work. A final answer alone does not establish the cause; a diagnostic question can distinguish possible causes. Missing evidence remains unknown. The numerical-difference requirement is a protocol choice, not a feedback rule validated by these studies. |
| Verify the criterion | Huang et al. (2024) found limitations of intrinsic self-correction on the models and reasoning tasks studied. This is model-reliability evidence, not evidence of learning gains. | Reference answers, examples, or criteria are checked before judgment with calculation, execution, a source, or the task requirements. Unresolved criteria qualify the dependent judgment. This checking policy is a design response to reliability limits, not a demonstrated guarantee of correctness. |
| Select support | Wood et al. (1976) describe tutoring functions; Kalyuga et al. (2003) qualify guidance by expertise; Renkl & Atkinson (2003) discuss transition from examples to problem solving. | A cue, worked step, or simpler task addresses the observed need. The learner then attempts meaningful work; experienced learners can proceed without redundant explanation. |
| Organize an explanation | Chandler & Sweller (1991) studied split-source instructional information. Pollock et al. (2002) studied isolated elements followed by their interactions in complex materials. | Dependent information is available together when needed. Separate component instruction returns to their relationships; the next task requires using those relationships. For submitted work, the skill starts from the learner's result or decision and uses a brief contrast when useful. This explanation order is a design choice; the studies do not establish that order or imply presenting all information at once. |
| Adjust support | van de Pol et al. (2010) distinguish contingent support, fading, and transfer of responsibility. Their review also identifies limits in the evidence and measurement of scaffolding. | The next attempt indicates whether support was used successfully. Errors prompt another representation, prerequisite support, or diagnostic task; improved performance permits less help. A brief task-relevant explanation can make a support change understandable (Nielsen, 1994/2024). Exact difficulty changes and explanation timing remain design choices. |
| Recall | Roediger & Karpicke (2006) found delayed retention benefits from testing studied prose. Kornell et al. (2009) found benefits from unsuccessful attempts followed by answers in their experimental materials. | Recall precedes re-exposure when an attempt is useful. Failed recall receives checked information and another usable opportunity; giving information is not counted as successful learner retrieval. |
| Method selection | Brunmair & Richter (2019) synthesized 59 interleaving studies and found substantial moderation by learning material. Some materials benefited; word materials favored blocking on average. | Confusable cases are compared and mixed when initial understanding allows; selection of an appropriate method provides evidence. The compare-first, mix-later sequence is a design choice, not an order established by this meta-analysis. |
| Coverage | Anderson et al. (1994) found that selective retrieval could impair later recall of related, unpractised category items under their experimental conditions. This does not establish that all narrow practice harms learning. | Important unpractised cases are sampled within the chosen capability. After component practice, whole-task use checks whether the learner can combine the units (Merrill, 2002). Component success alone does not establish the whole capability. The sampling and recombination policy is a design inference. |
| Retention practice | Cepeda et al. (2008) studied spacing as a function of the intended retention interval. Their results do not establish one universally optimal schedule. | A future-use goal leads to a delayed retrieval plan, with the gap chosen for the retention horizon and observed performance. A later attempt supplies outcome evidence; a plan does not establish retention or a scheduled reminder. |

## Interaction and evidence decisions

| Node | Research or disciplinary basis | Decision and observable check |
|---|---|---|
| First use | Horvitz (1999) proposes resolving important goal uncertainty through efficient dialogue while preserving user control. Amershi et al. (2019) recommend making capabilities clear at initial interaction; Nielsen (1994/2024) supports familiar language and recognition. | Instructions alone lead to a short role introduction and one goal question. A supplied task leads to useful work without a compulsory mode choice or control list. A relevant help option can be mentioned when useful; the full controls and their meanings appear on request. This uses Nielsen's recognition and minimalist-design guidance. The exact wording, display policy, and question count are design choices; novice usability is not established by these sources. |
| Mode selection, control display, Menu, and natural language | Nielsen (1994/2024) provides usability guidance on visible status, recognition, consistency, flexibility, and user control. | The request selects Learn, Practice, or Check; the activity is described in the learner's language and English aliases remain retrievable. Menu leaves the pending task intact. Exact labels, grouping, English control names, and display frequency are implementation choices. |
| Shared next step | Amershi et al. (2019), guidelines 11 and 16, address explaining system behavior and conveying how user actions affect subsequent behavior. Nielsen (1994/2024) supports visible status and user control. | Purpose and division of work are made clear when a new step needs explanation. With submitted work, feedback starts from the relevant result or decision; generic announcements and recaps are skipped when they add no teaching value. Feedback connects that work to the next support decision, and future checks identify the decisions to be assessed without revealing their answers. This tutoring exchange is a design choice, not evidence of improved learning; a plan alone demonstrates no learner performance. |
| Learn, Practice, Hint, and Example | Wood et al. (1976); Kalyuga et al. (2003); Renkl & Atkinson (2003). | Explanations and demonstrations supply needed knowledge; guided attempts reveal how the learner uses it. A cue leaves work to do. Receiving an example is distinguished from demonstrating a capability. |
| Check and assistance | Soderstrom & Bjork (2015) distinguish learning from temporary performance. Bastani et al. (2025) separately measured assisted practice and subsequent unassisted outcomes in high-school mathematics; it is not cited here as a test of delayed retention or transfer. | Attempts are classified by help available before or during them; later feedback does not change that record. Further work after task-specific help is assisted. In a multi-item Check, attempts precede instructional feedback; remaining items whose method is revealed are assisted. Exact transitions and timing are design choices. |
| Progress and status labels | Bull & Kay (2016) provide a framework for inspectable learner information. Soderstrom & Bjork (2015) constrain inferences from immediate performance. | Each relevant capability is accompanied by its evidence and assistance conditions, in a compact table or short lines suited to the learner's reading needs. Reports remain distinguishable from observed work, and contrary evidence remains visible. Retention and Transfer remain distinguishable even when both are unchecked. Status labels, including a pending judgment when required criteria cannot be verified, are descriptive conventions, not validated proficiency scales. |
| Retention and Transfer evidence | Butler (2010) examined delayed retention and transfer using new inferential questions. Roediger & Karpicke (2006) found different relative benefits at five minutes versus two days or one week. Soderstrom & Bjork (2015) review performance versus learning. | The delay or context change, cues, assistance, and outcome accompany the status. Failure or assisted success is not independent success. No universal delay or chat-session boundary is prescribed; an immediate retry cannot establish longer-term retention. |
| Direct requests, Stop, and completion | Patall et al. (2008) synthesized effects of choice and their moderators; Nielsen (1994/2024) supports user control. In contrast, Bastani et al. (2025) found worse subsequent unaided exam outcomes with unrestricted GPT access than in the control condition; their guarded tutor avoided that loss without showing a positive unaided effect. | Direct answers follow learner control, not a claim that answer access maximizes learning. Tutor output alone is not learner success. Stopping ends the session; completion states demonstrated outcomes and offers a purposeful next practice only if wanted. These conversational policies are design choices. |

## Skill structure and evidence limits

The Agent Skills specification requires a `SKILL.md` with name and description metadata; scripts and supporting directories are optional. Anthropic's authoring guidance emphasizes concise, relevant instructions and a degree of procedural specificity suited to the task. Mentor therefore keeps its operating decisions in one skill file, public usage in the README, scientific rationale here, and licensing in LICENSE. This division is a packaging choice informed by those sources, not a measured learning intervention.

Jane Street's [Socratic Tutor](https://github.com/janestreet/agent_skills/blob/7c6715c29548a3b39d4a9545c698ee4fd17a7213/socratic-tutor/SKILL.md) is a design reference for a compact state note carried by the learner. Mentor's note retains task evidence, assistance, provenance, and timing, and is treated as reported history in a new chat. This is a usability design choice, not evidence of learning gains.

Format validation checks the package; scripted model runs check particular decisions and outputs. A generated question can have a correct reference answer yet fail to distinguish strategies: the learner's mistaken rule may still produce the right answer. Diagnostic task selection needs checking separately from answer correctness. A new question alone is not evidence that a misunderstanding has been resolved.

The cited studies concern particular learners, tasks, materials, or models. They do not establish the effectiveness of this complete protocol across domains, the optimality of its interface, universal model reliability, or persistent learning inside the AI model. Human learning outcomes require evidence from learners under the claimed conditions.
